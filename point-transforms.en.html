00 / BEFORE CALCULATING

Same point. A different coordinate frame.

In this note, a coordinate transform keeps the point fixed in space and expresses it in another frame. This differs from an active rotation, which moves the point while keeping the reference axes fixed. The equations can look alike, so identify the frames and subscripts first.

W / C
W is the world frame; C is the camera frame. The camera uses right-handed axes: x right, y down, z forward.
pW / pC
The same point expressed in world / camera coordinates. All vectors are column vectors, also written as (1, 0, 4)ᵀ.
RWC
A rotation matrix that converts a vector expressed in C into its representation in W.
tWC
The camera origin expressed in world coordinates. Point coordinates and translations are measured in meters.

The subscripts mean “output frame, input frame.” In homogeneous-transform notation, TWC = TW←C, or T_W_from_C.

pC =104
RWC =0−10100001
tWC =120

The transform determines the world-axis directions. We do not impose an additional convention such as world y pointing vertically upward.

01 / CAMERA → WORLD

Align the axes, then add the origin.

pW = RWC pC + tWC

RWC pC is the vector from the camera origin to the point, now expressed along the world axes. Adding the camera origin’s world position, tWC, gives the point’s position relative to the world origin.

① Apply the rotation matrix. Each component is a row–column dot product.

RWC pC =0·1 − 1·0 + 0·41·1 + 0·0 + 0·40·1 + 0·0 + 1·4= 014

② Add the origin’s position.

pW =014+ 120= 134

The point has not moved. Its camera coordinates are (1, 0, 4)ᵀ, and its world coordinates are (1, 3, 4)ᵀ.

02 / WORLD → CAMERA

Subtract the translation, then undo the rotation.

Rearrange the original equation to isolate pC. For a rotation matrix, the inverse is its transpose: R⁻¹ = Rᵀ.

pW − tWC = RWC pC
pC = RWCᵀ (pW − tWC)
= RWCᵀ pW − RWCᵀ tWC

To write the inverse as pC = RCW pW + tCW, compute both of these terms:

RCW = RWCᵀtCW = −RCW tWC

Negating the translation alone is not enough. tCW is “the world origin expressed in camera coordinates.” It is expressed in a different frame from tWC, so it must also be rotated into that frame.

RCW =010−100001
tCW = −2−10= −210

First compute RCW pW on its own. Its second component is −1 × 1 + 0 × 3 + 0 × 4 = −1. Do not carry over the original y-coordinate of 3.

pC =3−14+ −210= 104

The original coordinates are recovered. As another check, first calculate pW − tWC = (0, 1, 4)ᵀ, then apply the inverse rotation.

03 / CAMERA → IMAGE

Turn coordinate ratios into pixels.

Use a pinhole camera without lens distortion. Projection takes camera coordinates, pC = (XC, YC, ZC)ᵀ. A point given in world coordinates must first be transformed into the camera frame.

Image u increases to the right, and v downward. The focal lengths are fx = fy = 400; the principal point is (cx, cy) = (320, 240). These values and the image coordinates are measured in pixels.

u = fx XCZC + cxv = fy YCZC + cy

ZC is depth along the optical axis, not the Euclidean distance √(XC² + YC² + ZC²) from the camera to the point. This example uses points in front of the camera, with ZC > 0. At zero the formula is undefined; a negative value places the point behind the camera.

Project the point (1, 0, 4)ᵀ:

u = 400 × 1/4 + 320 = 420v = 400 × 0/4 + 240 = 240

Double the point’s coordinates to (2, 0, 8)ᵀ:

u = 400 × 2/8 + 320 = 420v = 400 × 0/8 + 240 = 240

Both give (u, v) = (420, 240). The image position is unchanged. Since 1/4 = 2/8, points in the same direction from the camera project to the same pixel even when their depths differ.

Can image coordinates alone determine depth?

A single image coordinate does not determine a unique depth. Even with known camera intrinsics, it gives the ratios XC/ZC and YC/ZC. Scaling every component of the point by the same positive λ leaves both ratios unchanged.

u − cx = fx XCZC⇒ ZC = fx XCu − cx

This rearrangement gives depth only when XC is known from other information and u ≠ cx. With image coordinates alone, XC is unknown too. Rearranging an equation does not add depth information.

04 / THREE PLACES I GOT STUCK

Turn sticking points into useful checks.

01

Does inverse translation just change the sign?

The form pC = RCWpW + tCW is correct. Its terms need to be derived correctly. Working backward from the original equation—subtract translation, then apply inverse rotation—gives tCW = −RWCᵀtWC.

02

The recovered point became (1, 4, 4)ᵀ.

Check the second component. The second row–column dot product in RCWpW is −1 × 1 + 0 × 3 + 0 × 4 = −1. Adding the translation’s +1 gives 0. Calculate each row instead of retaining the original y-coordinate and using 3 + 1.

03

I dropped fx from the depth formula.

In ZC = XC/(u − cx), the numerator should be fxXC. Writing the intermediate step (u − cx)ZC = fxXC helps keep every coefficient in the rearrangement.

05 / YOUR 45-MINUTE PRACTICE

Hide the equations. Explain them again.

This is a self-study checklist for you, the reader. Use pen and paper or a short program to work through the same numbers. If you spend three minutes stuck on one question, record it and move on.

  1. 0–5 min
    Write the frames and values.

    Note the C → W direction, column vectors, camera axes, and units. Copy pC, RWC, and tWC.

  2. 5–15 min
    Transform into world coordinates.

    Calculate rotation and translation separately. Explain whether the point moved or its representation changed.

  3. 15–25 min
    Recover the point with the inverse.

    Rearrange the original equation to derive RCW and tCW. Check that you recover pC. Explain in one sentence why negating the translation is insufficient.

  4. 25–37 min
    Project two points into the image.

    Project (1, 0, 4)ᵀ and (2, 0, 8)ᵀ, and compare the results. Explain whether image coordinates alone determine depth.

  5. 37–45 min
    Explain it in two minutes; record what was difficult.

    Hide the equations and explain “forward transform → inverse transform → projection.” Record up to three sticking points using the prompts below.

  • Where did you get stuck?
  • What was unclear?
  • How do you understand it now?

You do not need to fill all three slots. Beyond getting the numbers right, the goal is to explain which frame each quantity belongs to and why the operations occur in that order.
